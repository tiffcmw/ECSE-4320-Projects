# 5. Experiment 4: intensity sweep, knee and Little's Law

**Hypotheses**: H4a to H4f, H11. Expected: one core sustains a plateau of outstanding misses; chip read bandwidth saturates at 55 to 85% of 460 GB/s; loaded latency stays within 1.3 times idle up to about half the saturation bandwidth and then rises; the number of lines in flight, N = bandwidth times latency divided by 128 B, is 200 to 700 at the knee.

**Setup.** (a) One thread runs K independent random chains (K = 1 to 64): throughput should grow linearly with K until a limit of outstanding misses, and N_eff = throughput times the K = 1 latency divided by 128 B tests Little's Law where the true count K is known. (b) A loaded-latency harness runs N sequential-read threads (no injected delay, 1 to 8 threads in steps of one, then 10, 12, 14 and 16) while a separate probe thread measures latency by a random chase over 1 GiB. (c) Injection delay: 16 threads and 6 threads execute a busy loop of the stated length between 1 MiB slices, which lowers the offered load. Each bandwidth thread streams a private 256 MiB buffer. 12 rounds.

**Knee definitions, fixed before the analysis.** K1: the smallest thread count whose median bandwidth reaches 95% of the maximum median bandwidth of the thread sweep. K2: the point of the whole curve (thread sweep and both delay series) with the largest bandwidth divided by latency. Both are reported because a knee defined by one rule alone can be an artifact of that rule. The 1.5 times idle criterion used elsewhere is not used as a knee.

## 6.1 The plateau between L1 and L2

The latency between about 144 KiB and 1.1 MiB is {{v['plateau']|.1f}} ns, which neither the 128 KiB L1 nor the 16 MiB L2 explains. A dependent chase that repeats a fixed address sequence could in principle be helped by a load-address or value predictor with a capacity of several thousand entries; 1.1 MiB at 128 B per node is about 9000 nodes. Two tests separate "bytes" from "node count":

![Figure 8. Plateau tests. (a) Latency versus footprint in bytes for node spacings of 64, 128 and 256 B. (b) The same data against the number of nodes. (c) Plain random cycle versus a mutating walk whose next address is a hash of the value just loaded, with a fresh value written at each visit, so the address sequence never repeats. Median and min to max over 9 rounds.](../plots/exp5b_plateau_tests.png){width=90%}

(a) With 64, 128 and 256 B spacing the nodes differ by a factor of four in number at the same footprint. The step up from L1 (135 to 160 KiB) and the step to the L2 level (between 0.9 and 1.3 MiB) are at the same footprints in bytes for all three spacings and not at the same node counts (Figure 8b) *(measured)*. A predictor with a fixed number of node entries is therefore contradicted. (b) The mutating walk shows no flat plateau, but it also differs statistically from the cycle (it visits nodes with replacement, so a cache-like level of fixed size produces a gradual rise of the hit fraction, not a step). Its latency rises smoothly from {{nr('e12_plateau','ns_per_load',49152,exp='e12_mut')|.1f}} ns at 48 KiB to {{nr('e12_plateau','ns_per_load',1.06*2**20,exp='e12_mut')|.1f}} ns at 1.1 MiB. This test cannot separate the explanations and is inconclusive. The plateau is reported as an unexplained cache-like level that follows capacity in bytes *(measured)*; its identity is not established.

# 7. Experiment 6: effect of cache misses on kernels

# 8. Experiment 7: TLB-miss impact

**Hypotheses**: H8a to H8d. Each pitfall is shown with the misleading result next to the corrected one (Figure 11).

**1. Cache-resident buffers.** A 16-thread read test with 256 KiB per thread (4 MiB total) reports {{v['res4']|.0f}} GB/s; the same test at 256 MiB total reports {{v['res256']|.0f}} GB/s, the DRAM value. The misleading value is {{v['res4']/v['res256']|.1f}}x too high and exceeds the 460 GB/s peak, which flags it as impossible for DRAM (H8a).

**2. Prefetchable chase.** A sequential chase over a 1 GiB footprint reports {{v['seq1g']|.1f}} ns per load against {{v['DRAM']|.0f}} ns for the random chase, {{v['DRAM']/v['seq1g']|.0f}}x too low (H8b).

**3. Dead-code elimination.** A read loop whose result is never used has no symbol in the compiled program (the optimizer deleted it; Appendix E shows the call site: only the sinking variant is called). Timing it claims {{v['nosink']|.2g}} GB/s, against {{v['sink']|.1f}} GB/s once the result is summed into a sink (H8c).

**4. Clock ramp.** A process that has been idle for 3 s runs its first 5 ms at {{100*v['ramp5']|.0f}}% of the steady clock, 20 ms at {{100*v['ramp20']|.0f}}%, 50 ms at {{100*v['ramp50']|.0f}}% and reaches the steady {{v['steady']|.2f}} GHz after about 100 ms. A benchmark that times a short region right after idle underestimates the speed by up to a factor of two (H8d: the 1.1x slowdown predicted is far exceeded). This is why every process spins for 300 ms first.
