# 2. Experiment 1: zero-queue baselines

**Hypotheses** (Appendix C): H1a to H1e, HV1, HV2. Expected: L1 3 to 5 cycles, L2 12 to 40 cycles, a latency ramp (not a plateau) for the system-level cache, DRAM 90 to 130 ns, latency non-decreasing with footprint.

**What was measured.** Time per dependent load for one thread chasing a single random cycle over footprints from 4 KiB to 2 GiB (an otherwise idle machine, one thread, no other memory traffic), after a warm pass, 12 rounds, each round with a new random permutation.

The 4 MiB point spans 256 pages and likely includes a small translation cost (Exp 7 shows +1.3 ns for 256 pages with one line per page), so the 2 MiB value, {{cyc(v['L2_2M'])|.0f}} cycles (estimated), is the better L2 figure *(inferred)*. At 12 MiB the latency is {{v['L2_12M']|.1f}} ns, in the ramp toward the system-level cache. DRAM latency at 1 GiB is {{v['DRAM']|.1f}} ns ({{cyc(v['DRAM'])|.0f}} cycles, estimated); the separate loaded-latency harness measures {{v['idle']|.1f}} ns with no bandwidth threads.

**Level edges (from the curve, no counters).** The first footprint above 1.5 times the L1 plateau is {{fb(v['edgeL1'])}} (sysctl capacity 128 KiB). The ramp toward the system-level cache starts at {{v['edgeL2']/2**20|.1f}} MiB, below the 16 MiB L2 capacity, and the curve reaches within 10% of the DRAM value at {{v['edgeDRAM']/2**20|.0f}} MiB. Between those points the latency rises smoothly; an SLC capacity cannot be read from it and none is claimed. A single thread cannot use the whole 16 MiB L2 at full speed; why is not established.

**Plateau between L1 and L2.** Between about 144 KiB and 1.1 MiB the latency is flat at about {{v['plateau']|.1f}} ns, well below the L2 value. It is not explained by a cache listed in `sysctl`. Section 6 tests two explanations.

**Caveat.** Cycle values depend on the frequency estimate. The 1 GiB point includes translation cost, because the footprint spans 65536 pages.

# 3. Experiment 2: access pattern and granularity

**Hypotheses**: H2a to H2f. Expected: a sequential chase is at least 5 times faster than a random one; a random chase does not depend on node stride; the prefetcher stops helping at large strides; useful bandwidth of a 16 B access at a stride of 128 B or more is about 1/8 of the line rate; and the penalty at 128 B and 256 B strides does not depend on power-of-two alignment.

**Setup.** (a) A pointer chase whose nodes are `stride` bytes apart, in sequential order or in random order, for strides 64 B to 4 KiB. The number of distinct lines is held at 1 Mi (the footprint is the larger of 1 GiB and 1 Mi times the stride) so that a larger stride never shrinks the cache footprint. (b) Independent loads (not a chain) of 16, 32, 64 or 128 B at the same strides, sequential or random slots, one thread, with eight independent address streams. (c) Aliasing tests: independent random 16 B loads at strides 128, 192, 256, 320, 384, 512, 640 and 1024 B, with and without a hashed line offset inside each stride block, and the two arithmetic kernels of Section 7 at the same strides.


![Figure 2. Pattern and granularity. (a) Dependent chase versus node stride, sequential and random order. (b, c) Useful bandwidth of independent loads of four access sizes versus stride, sequential and random slots. Median, bars: minimum to maximum over rounds (n = 5 for the chase, n = 9 for the independent loads). Fixed: one thread, footprint of at least 1 GiB. Varied: stride, order, access size.](../plots/exp2_pattern_granularity.png){width=90%}

**Results.** A sequential chase with 128 B nodes measures {{v['seq128']|.1f}} ns per node against {{v['rand128']|.1f}} ns for the random chase, {{v['rand128']/v['seq128']|.1f}}x lower *(measured)*. The random chase does not depend on node stride: 108 to 114 ns over 64 B to 4 KiB (the 4 KiB value is {{100*(v['rand4096']/v['rand128']-1)|.0f}}% higher, plausibly the translation cost at 4 GiB) *(measured)*. The prefetch benefit of the sequential chase persists up to a 1 KiB stride ({{v['seq1024']|.1f}} ns) and is gone at 2 KiB ({{v['seq2048']|.0f}} ns) and 4 KiB ({{v['seq4096']|.0f}} ns), so the hardware tracks strides of at most 1 KiB in this test *(measured)*. The value at 1 KiB ({{v['seq1024']|.1f}} ns) is lower than at 256 B and 512 B ({{v['seq256']|.1f}} and {{v['seq512']|.1f}} ns); this non-monotonic behavior is unexplained (Section 11). Independent sequential loads of a whole line reach {{v['pat_seq_128_128']|.0f}} GB/s at stride 128 B, close to the one-core read bandwidth of Section 6; the same loads at a 256 B stride reach {{v['pat_seq_128_256']|.0f}} GB/s and at 1 KiB {{v['pat_seq_128_1024']|.0f}} GB/s. A 16 B access at a 128 B stride yields {{v['pat_seq_16_128']|.2f}} GB/s sequentially and {{v['pat_rand_16_128']|.2f}} GB/s randomly, so the useful rate is about 1/{{v['pat_seq_128_128']/v['pat_seq_16_128']|.0f}} and 1/{{v['pat_rand_128_128']/v['pat_rand_16_128']|.1f}} of the same stream with whole-line accesses, not the 1/8 predicted (H2e).

**Interpretation.** *(inferred)* Because sequential access is fast only while the stride is within the prefetcher's reach, a "latency" taken from a sequential or small-stride chase measures streaming, not memory latency (Exp 8). *(inferred)* The granularity of memory traffic is the 128 B line. Random 16 B loads complete {{v['pat_rand_16_128']*1e9/16/1e6|.0f}} million times per second, each moving a full line, which is {{v['pat_rand_16_128']*1e9/16*128/1e9|.1f}} GB/s of line traffic, equal to the one-core random-miss plateau of Section 5 ({{v['mlp_plateau']|.1f}} GB/s). Useful bandwidth of narrow random accesses is therefore the line rate times 16/128. Random whole-line (128 B) loads from the same kernel reach only {{v['pat_rand_128_128']|.1f}} GB/s of line traffic, about a third of that; the cause is unexplained (Section 11).

## 3.1 Stride aliasing test

The aliasing concern is that power-of-two strides map to few cache sets or memory channels and inflate the cost. Two things were varied: the stride itself (power of two or not) and a hashed offset of the access inside the stride block (which changes the address bits above the offset but keeps the 128 B alignment).

![Figure 3. Stride aliasing. (a) Independent random 16 B loads: useful bandwidth versus stride, plain and with a hashed line offset inside each stride block (median, bars: min to max, n = 9). (b, c) Arithmetic kernels z = x*y and y = a*x + y: ns per element versus element stride for three footprints; x markers: hashed offset (n = 7). Dotted vertical lines mark 128, 256, 512 and 1024 B.](../plots/exp2b_stride_aliasing.png){width=90%}


**Result.** The slow strides are the multiples of 128 B: 128, 256, 384, 512, 640 and 1024 B give 2.8 to 3.2 GB/s, while 192 B ({{v['al192']|.1f}} GB/s) and 320 B ({{v['al320']|.1f}} GB/s) are about {{v['al192']/v['al128']|.1f}} times faster, although neither is a power of two. 384 B and 640 B are not powers of two and are as slow as 128 B. The penalty therefore follows "stride is a multiple of the 128 B line" (every access lands on the first 16 B of a line), not power-of-two alignment *(measured)*. The hashed line offset changes the power-of-two strides by {{100*(v['alj256']/v['al256']-1)|.0f}}% (256 B), {{100*(v['alj512']/v['al512']-1)|.0f}}% (512 B) and {{100*(v['alj1024']/v['al1024']-1)|.0f}}% (1024 B), so channel or set aliasing between lines is not the cause of the 128 B and 256 B slowdown *(measured; aliasing between lines is not supported as the cause)*. The kernels show the same pattern: at the 1.5 GiB footprint the z = x*y kernel costs {{v['kmul128']|.1f}} ns per element at a 128 B stride and {{v['kmul384']|.1f}} ns at 384 B, but only {{v['kmul192']|.1f}} ns at 192 B and {{v['kmul320']|.1f}} ns at 320 B. Why half-line-offset strides run faster is not established. A plausible reading is that accesses at an offset of 64 B inside a line are served differently from accesses at offset 0 (speculation, untested).

# 4. Experiment 3: read/write mix

**Hypotheses**: H3a to H3e. Expected: read-only is fastest; non-temporal stores change little; `dc zva` is faster than plain stores; a write-allocate model corrects 100% write toward the read rate; store throughput depends on the data stored.

**Setup.** One thread streams through 10-line groups, reading R lines from one buffer and writing W lines to another: 100% read, 70/30, 50/50, 100% write, with plain stores, with non-temporal pair stores (`stnp`), with `dc zva`, and a read-modify-write mix on the same lines. Footprints 256 KiB, 8 MiB and 1 GiB (9 rounds). The store data is random (a per-line pseudo-random sequence) unless stated; variants with a constant value per line ("uniform") and with zero data test the content dependence. A loaded variant runs the same mixes on 0 to 16 threads with the probe thread measuring latency. `dc zva` zeroes a block without reading it; the block size read from `DCZID_EL0` is 64 B, so two instructions clear one 128 B line. "Application GB/s" counts the bytes the program reads and writes. "Modeled DRAM GB/s" multiplies application bytes of plain stores by a write-allocate factor and is a model, not a measurement.

**Results.** Mixed traffic is faster than read-only for one thread: 70/30 reaches {{v['mix_r7w3_st_r']|.0f}} GB/s and 50/50 {{v['mix_r5w5_st_r']|.0f}} GB/s against {{v['mix_r10w0']|.0f}} GB/s for reads alone, and 100% write with plain stores reaches {{v['mix_r0w10_st_r']|.0f}} GB/s ({{v['mix_r0w10_st_r']/v['mix_r10w0']|.2f}} of read). The ordering assumed in H3a (read fastest) does not hold. Read-modify-write reaches {{v['mix_rmw']|.0f}} GB/s. Non-temporal stores of random data are {{v['mix_r0w10_nt_r']/v['mix_r0w10_st_r']|.2f}}x faster than plain stores for 100% write.

**The data content matters.** Plain stores of zero lines run at {{v['mix_r0w10_st_z']|.0f}} GB/s, {{v['mix_r0w10_st_z']/v['mix_r0w10_st_r']|.1f}} times the rate for random data, and `stnp` of uniform non-zero lines runs at {{v['mix_r0w10_nt']|.0f}} GB/s against {{v['mix_r0w10_nt_r']|.0f}} GB/s for random lines. `dc zva`, which stores no data at all, reaches {{v['mix_r0w10_zva']|.0f}} GB/s, the same as zero-data plain stores. Single-thread read bandwidth does not depend on buffer content (loaded read runs with random buffers: {{g('e11_ceiling','bw_gbps',exp='e11_fillrand',variant='r10w0',threads=4)|.0f}} and {{g('e11_ceiling','bw_gbps',exp='e11_fillrand',variant='r10w0',threads=6)|.0f}} GB/s at 4 and 6 threads, against {{v['bw4']|.0f}} and {{v['bw6']|.0f}} GB/s). *(inferred)* Zero lines and uniform lines are handled more cheaply somewhere in the write path (for example detected and not written in full); a rate of {{v['mix_r0w10_zva']|.0f}} GB/s from one core is therefore not DRAM write bandwidth, and neither the `dc zva` result nor the uniform `stnp` result can be used as evidence for or against write-allocate traffic. The mechanism is untested.

**Write-allocate.** With random data, plain 100% write moves {{v['mix_r0w10_st_r']|.0f}} GB/s of application bytes; a model in which each stored line is first fetched would put the DRAM traffic at {{2*v['mix_r0w10_st_r']|.0f}} GB/s, more than the one-core read rate of {{v['mix_r10w0']|.0f}} GB/s. The data neither confirm nor exclude write-allocate: the direct test (zero-fill without fetch) is confounded by content, and `stnp` with random data is only {{100*(v['mix_r0w10_nt_r']/v['mix_r0w10_st_r']-1)|.0f}}% faster than plain stores, not the factor of about 2 that removing a fetch would give. A write-allocate factor of two cannot be read from these data. *(speculation)* Plain stores may be limited by the store buffer or by write-back bandwidth rather than by an allocate fetch.

**Why mixed traffic beats read-only for one core.** One core's read rate is limited by the number of demand misses it can keep in flight (Exp 4: about 19 random-line misses, while sequential streams reach about 80 lines in flight through the prefetcher). Stores retire into a store buffer and drain through a different path, so adding stores raises the total bytes moved without consuming more read slots. This reading is consistent with the data (read-modify-write, which needs both paths, is fastest at {{v['mix_rmw']|.0f}} GB/s); it is not proven.

**Loaded runs.** On 16 threads, 100% read reaches {{v['r16']|.0f}} GB/s with a probe latency of {{g('e3_mix_loaded','lat_ns',variant='r10w0',threads=16)|.0f}} ns, read-modify-write reaches {{v['rmw16']|.0f}} GB/s at {{v['rmw16lat']|.0f}} ns, 70/30 plain stores reach {{g('e3_mix_loaded','bw_gbps',variant='r7w3_st_r',threads=16)|.0f}} GB/s with a probe latency of {{g('e3_mix_loaded','lat_ns',variant='r7w3_st_r',threads=16)|.0f}} ns ({{g('e3_mix_loaded','lat_ns',variant='r7w3_st_r',threads=16)/v['idle']|.1f}}x idle), and 100% write with plain stores {{g('e3_mix_loaded','bw_gbps',variant='r0w10_st_r',threads=16)|.0f}} GB/s. Write-heavy mixes give lower aggregate throughput and higher read latency than read-only, but because their rates differ the comparison of H3d (equal application rate) was not performed.

**Caveat.** The Table 7 rows with large min-to-max ranges (50/50 plain stores) include rounds disturbed by background activity; medians are used.
